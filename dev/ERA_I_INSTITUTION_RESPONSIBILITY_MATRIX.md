# Design Investigation 03 — Era I Institution and Responsibility Matrix

**Status:** Historical/design proposal for Game Director review; not approved GDD canon  
**Date:** 2026-11-23  
**Scope:** Institutional settings, authority, resources, records, orientation contact, and playable administrative problems in Uruk c. 3200 BCE  
**Authority:** `docs/GDD.md` remains canonical. This document does not revise the GDD.  
**Implementation:** None. No production code, save schema, or runtime content is changed.

## 1. Executive recommendation

Build Era I around **overlapping institutional households, precincts, administrative responsibilities, and exchange networks**, not four mutually exclusive modern departments.

The most defensible opening landscape is:
1. **Eanna precinct and its associated institutional administration** — the strongest direct setting for tablets, sealings, storage, distribution, production-related activity, and ceremonial/public gatherings.
2. **Anu/Kullaba monumental precinct** — a distinct sacred/monumental setting, but with a much thinner direct administrative record than Eanna. Do not invent a parallel archive or assume its precise divine dedication and internal organization are certain.
3. **Institutional households and work groups** — the people and dependencies through which goods, labor, and provisions are organized. Treat these as social/organizational structures rather than modern nuclear households or a single bureaucracy.
4. **Agricultural and pastoral provisioning networks** — fields, herds, food production, storage, and the movement of staples from the surrounding landscape into the city.
5. **Craft and processing activities** — production and transformation of goods, some potentially connected to large institutions, while the degree of direct administrative control varies by commodity and context.
6. **Exchange and procurement networks** — movement of raw materials and finished goods across local, regional, and interregional routes. Do not automatically call every participant a private merchant.
7. **Collective labor and public/monumental works** — people, materials, and provisioning assembled for construction, maintenance, and other large tasks. Specific labor obligations and command structures remain scenario-specific reconstructions.
8. **Security, coercion, and armed activity** — visible in limited iconographic evidence, but not sufficiently documented to posit a standing army, a military bureaucracy, or a standard military scribal career.

The four GDD professional orientations remain player-facing **professional emphases**. Civic and Religious should overlap heavily in the institutional world; Commercial should focus on movement and exchange relationships; Martial should remain provisional and low-confidence in Era I, represented through specific security, movement, and mobilization matters rather than a fabricated army institution.

## 2. Evidence and confidence rules

Use these labels consistently in authoring, historical notes, and QA:

- **Attested:** A relevant object, text, architectural feature, or image is directly present in the archaeological record for the period. Attestation of an object does not automatically prove its interpretation.
- **Inferred:** A cautious interpretation supported by multiple evidence classes or a scholarly reconstruction that is plausible but not directly recorded.
- **Reconstruction:** A specific person, office, procedure, dispute, obligation, or playable event authored to turn the evidence into a human scenario. It must not be presented as a documented historical event or universal practice.
- **Unsupported / reject:** The claim exceeds the available evidence or imports a later institution, title, or practice without justification.

### Chronological caution

The opening date c. 3200 BCE falls around the Late Uruk / Uruk IV–III transition. Chronological schemes vary, and archaeological dating of Uruk IV material is not exact. Nissen notes that much of the relevant material was recovered from refuse deposits reused in building foundations rather than from secure use contexts. The best-excavated evidence is concentrated in Uruk IVa; earlier phases are much less fully known.

The design should not make an exact date carry more certainty than the archaeological record permits. If the game later spans the transition into Uruk III/Jemdet Nasr, content should identify which phase supports a claim.

## 3. Institutional and organizational matrix

### A. Eanna precinct and associated institutional administration

**Type:** Monumental precinct containing multiple activities and institutional functions; not a single fully reconstructed office or modern department.

**Evidence and functions**
- Large numbers of archaic tablets, sealings, and other administrative devices are associated with Eanna.
- Evidence supports administrative activity, storage/distribution, large-scale ceremonial or public gatherings, and possible craft activity.
- Accounts concern animal husbandry, fields, cereal production, bread/beer, food allocations, and workforce categories.
- The archaeological context is imperfect: assemblages were often discarded, reused, or recovered without a complete functional context. Building-by-building assignments are unsafe.

**Authority and people**
- Use a layered group of officials, administrators, specialists, and workers connected to institutional activity.
- The archaic lexical lists include titles/functions and professions, but their exact readings and organizational relationships are debated.
- Avoid assigning every responsibility to a single “chief priest,” “mayor,” or “temple manager.”
- A high-status authority figure may appear in ritual, distribution, and public scenes, but the exact office and constitutional structure should remain uncertain.

**Resources and flows**
- Grain and other cereal products; bread and beer; animals and animal products; fields and yields; stored goods; crafted products; imported prestige/raw materials; labor and provisions.
- Do not assume that every workshop or producer was directly owned or managed by Eanna.

**Records and evidence**
- Proto-cuneiform tablets with quantities and commodity/category signs.
- Numerical-only tablets and tablets with columns, subtotals, or totals.
- Tags associated with containers.
- Clay sealings on doors, bales, and vessels; seal impressions and other accounting devices.
- Lexical lists of professions, titles, and categories.

**Orientation relevance**
- **Civic:** very high. Institutional coordination, records, storage, resource movement, and workforce matters.
- **Religious:** very high. Cultic/public activity, offerings and ceremonial goods where scenario evidence supports them, and the administration of an institution whose sacred and economic functions overlap.
- **Commercial:** medium-high. Procurement, delivery, imported materials, and external counterparties.
- **Martial:** low/conditional. Only where a specific security, transport, or mobilization matter is authored and evidenced.

**Playable administrative problems**
1. A tablet total does not reconcile with separate commodity entries.
2. A sealed container arrives with a mismatch between its tag, contents, and expected recipient.
3. A storeroom closure has been opened, damaged, or resealed; the evidence establishes a custody question but not automatically the culprit.
4. An outgoing allocation is recorded, but the final recipient or purpose is unclear.
5. A field, herd, or production account must be compared with the goods received into storage.
6. A ceremonial gathering or institutional distribution requires goods whose quantities, categories, or timing are disputed.
7. Two administrative units use different category conventions or numerical systems, making a superficial comparison misleading.

**Confidence:** Attested for administrative materials and multiple activities; inferred for specific internal jurisdictions and authority chains; reconstructed for named officials, particular disputes, and precise workflow.

### B. Anu/Kullaba monumental precinct

**Type:** Separate monumental/sacred precinct, not a second Eanna-style archive by default.

**Evidence and functions**
- The White Temple and its high terrace belong to the Late Uruk architectural landscape; the White Temple is commonly dated around the mid-fourth millennium BCE.
- The precinct is conventionally called the Anu precinct or Anu ziggurat area, but the temple's precise dedication is not securely established by direct material evidence.
- The White Temple is interpreted as a sanctuary. The god or gods worshipped there are not certain.
- Unlike Eanna, the excavated Kullaba/Anu area has not yielded a comparable corpus of proto-cuneiform tablets.

**Authority and people**
- A sacred precinct and people responsible for its upkeep, ritual activity, and associated material needs are reasonable world elements.
- Do not invent a fully documented Anu priesthood hierarchy, separate treasury, or archive.
- Do not assume it was institutionally subordinate or superior to Eanna.

**Resources and flows**
- Building maintenance, valuable construction materials, offerings or ceremonial goods as authored scenarios, and the people involved in sanctuary activity.
- Specific daily feeding/clothing schedules, herds, or estate accounts require evidence beyond the mere existence of a sanctuary.

**Records and evidence**
- The absence of a comparable excavated tablet archive is itself a design constraint.
- Use material evidence, oral instructions, delivery tags, sealings, and records held by connected institutions only when the scenario establishes them.
- Do not fabricate a surviving Anu administrative archive for the tutorial or ordinary Era I gameplay.

**Orientation relevance**
- **Religious:** high as a sacred/monumental context, but the administrative mechanics and documentation are less directly evidenced than at Eanna.
- **Civic:** conditional, where shared construction, resources, or civic/institutional relationships are involved.
- **Commercial:** conditional, where materials or goods are procured or delivered.
- **Martial:** none by default.

**Playable administrative problems**
1. A delivery of building material or a valuable object has no complete written chain of custody.
2. A maintenance request competes with another institutional need.
3. An oral instruction and a physical delivery disagree; the player must establish what is actually evidenced.
4. A ceremonial requirement is clear, but the responsible party or resource source is not.

These are reconstructed scenarios. The player must not be told that a specific Uruk IV Anu office or procedure is archaeologically documented.

**Confidence:** Attested for monumental architecture; inferred for broad sacred function; uncertain for exact divine dedication and internal administration; reconstructed for all named offices and operational workflows.

### C. Institutional households, personnel, and work groups

**Type:** Social and organizational structures associated with central institutions; not a single generic household class.

**Evidence and functions**
- Late Uruk lexical lists enumerate many higher- and middle-level officials and professionals connected to central communal institutions.
- Administrative records also classify lower-status people and workers, including age/sex categories and people described by terms interpreted as dependents and/or enslaved persons.
- Food and other goods were allocated to some categories of personnel.
- The evidence does not justify projecting a modern nuclear family, a universal household form, or a clean employee/owner distinction onto every group.

**Authority and people**
- Officials and supervisors may be responsible for particular people, tasks, stores, or allocations.
- The status and legal condition of many recorded people are difficult to reconstruct. Avoid flattening dependents, enslaved people, household members, and workers into one interchangeable labor pool.
- A person's relationship to an institutional household can be a major part of their life history without being identical to kinship or private household membership.

**Resources and flows**
- People assigned to tasks; food and other provisions; tools and materials; work outputs; allocation and receipt of goods.

**Records and evidence**
- Human-resource lists and allocations.
- Age/sex classifications and categories of personnel.
- Food allotments, inventories, and task-related accounts.
- Oral instructions and situated knowledge remain important because early records are context-dependent and do not narrate full procedures.

**Orientation relevance**
- **Civic:** high, where institutional coordination and accountability are involved.
- **Religious:** high, where personnel serve a sacred or ceremonial institution.
- **Commercial:** medium, where people, goods, or obligations move between counterparties.
- **Martial:** low/conditional; do not interpret workforce categories as military muster categories.

**Playable administrative problems**
1. A person's allocation appears under an unexpected category.
2. Two lists classify the same group differently; the player must not assume the categories are equivalent.
3. A work group has received goods, but the record does not establish who accepted responsibility.
4. A dependent or worker is missing from an allocation; the record may reveal an administrative discrepancy without establishing whether the person is absent, transferred, or misclassified.
5. An obligation is disputed between a household and an institution.

**Confidence:** Attested for human-resource recording and allocations; inferred for institutional household organization; reconstructed for specific individual relationships and disputes.

### D. Agricultural and pastoral provisioning network

**Type:** Distributed production and supply relationship between city institutions, surrounding settlements, fields, herds, and storage points; not one central farm or office.

**Evidence and functions**
- A substantial part of Uruk's provisioning had to come from its surrounding area.
- Archaic texts include fields, plowing, yield accounts, animal husbandry, grain, and distributions of cereal products and beer.
- Nissen notes that surviving administrators' records often focus on entries and exits from warehouses, while rarely revealing the final recipients of outgoing goods and not identifying the origins of incoming goods.
- The relationship between city and hinterland is essential, but its detailed administrative mechanisms are poorly documented.

**Authority and people**
- Possible counterparties include institutional officials, field or herd managers, carriers, local producers, and recipients.
- Do not presume that all land was owned by one temple-state or that all producers were direct institutional employees.
- Avoid modern farm-management titles unless clearly marked as UI descriptions.

**Resources and flows**
- Grain and cereal products, bread, beer, sheep/goats and other animals, animal products, and seasonal agricultural output.
- Inputs, storage loss, spoilage, and delivery timing may be modeled as scenario circumstances, not as universal historical rates.

**Records and evidence**
- Field and yield accounts; animal accounts; commodity entries and exits; delivery tags; sealed containers; numerical/metrological notation.
- Distinguish the recorded quantity from the unrecorded route, source, and final destination.

**Orientation relevance**
- **Civic:** high, for institutional provisioning and allocation.
- **Religious:** high where the goods are directed to a cultic or ceremonial use.
- **Commercial:** high, for movement and exchange between producers, carriers, and recipients.
- **Martial:** conditional, for a specific traveling or security-related provision task, not a default military ration system.

**Playable administrative problems**
1. A recorded warehouse receipt cannot be reconciled with the field/yield account.
2. A delivery arrives after the period for which it was expected.
3. Two measures refer to different commodity systems and cannot be added directly.
4. A herd count and a delivery record disagree.
5. The account proves that goods entered storage but does not identify their original producer.
6. The storehouse record proves an issue but does not identify the final recipient.

**Confidence:** Attested for commodity categories and accounts; inferred for the precise supply chain and organizational relationships; reconstructed for named suppliers, route events, and specific disputes.

### E. Craft, processing, and production contexts

**Type:** Workshops and production relationships that may be institutionally connected, independently organized, or mixed.

**Evidence and functions**
- Late Uruk evidence indicates differentiated craft production and specialized activities, including ceramic technology, metallurgy, and building trades.
- Eanna assemblages suggest possible craft activity, but contextual limitations prevent confidently assigning every installation or product to a particular building or centrally managed workshop.
- Administrative texts and seal imagery can illuminate production and distribution, but do not prove that all ordinary production was under direct central control.
- The degree of administrative involvement likely varied by product and production arrangement.

**Authority and people**
- Craft specialists, assistants, supervisors, suppliers, and recipients are plausible actors.
- Do not assume a standardized factory, guild, or universal state workshop model.
- A workshop may have a relationship with an institution without being fully owned or directly managed by it.

**Resources and flows**
- Raw materials, tools, fuel, finished goods, work in progress, and provisions for people involved.
- Imported materials can be valuable and scarce; do not treat all metals, timber, or stones as locally available.

**Records and evidence**
- Commodity lists, issue/receipt records, inventories, sealings, tags, and material inspection.
- Production stages may be represented through scenario-authored documents where a historical basis is established.

**Orientation relevance**
- **Civic:** medium-high, where output is commissioned, allocated, or accounted for.
- **Religious:** medium-high, where goods are made for sacred or ceremonial use.
- **Commercial:** high, where materials or finished products are exchanged or transported.
- **Martial:** conditional, for a specific tool/equipment request only; do not assume an established armory.

**Playable administrative problems**
1. A finished quantity does not match the amount of raw material issued.
2. A delivery's material or quality differs from the agreed description.
3. Two parties dispute whether a damaged item was defective before or after handover.
4. A scarce imported material has been allocated to two competing purposes.
5. A workshop output is recorded, but its recipient or custody is unclear.

**Confidence:** Attested for differentiated production and materials; inferred for the specific organization and degree of institutional control; reconstructed for named workshops, contracts, and audits.

### F. Exchange, transport, and interregional procurement networks

**Type:** Networks of movement, procurement, delivery, and relationships across local, regional, and interregional contexts; not necessarily a unified merchant institution.

**Evidence and functions**
- Southern Mesopotamia lacked many important raw materials and relied on relationships with neighboring and more distant regions for timber, metals, stones, and other goods.
- Uruk-period material and settlement evidence indicate long-distance networks, including Uruk-related settlements and material connections beyond southern Mesopotamia.
- The precise administrative mechanisms behind many of these relationships are not documented. Nissen notes that texts provide only occasional references to foreign goods and little direct information about foreign relations.
- Stein emphasizes that Uruk's external networks operated in a competitive political landscape and cautions against assuming a highly centralized state controlling every exchange.

**Authority and people**
- Possible actors include local carriers, intermediaries, institutional agents, external counterparties, and people responsible for receipt or custody.
- Do not assume a later private merchant house, formal commercial law, or a single central trade ministry.
- The GDD Commercial orientation is an orientation toward exchange and movement, not proof of a standardized merchant profession.

**Resources and flows**
- Timber, metals, stones, prestige materials, animals, and other imported goods; local commodities moving outward; containers, loads, and transport animals.
- Exact provenance and route may be unknown even when the material itself is identifiable.

**Records and evidence**
- Commodity records, tags, sealings, delivery accounts, inventories, and records of goods entering/leaving institutional stores.
- Oral agreements and contextual knowledge can fill gaps, but they must remain distinguishable from written evidence.

**Orientation relevance**
- **Commercial:** very high.
- **Civic:** high where procurement or institutional resources are involved.
- **Religious:** medium where materials serve a precinct or ceremonial purpose.
- **Martial:** low/conditional, where security or escort is explicitly part of a scenario.

**Playable administrative problems**
1. A delivery arrives without a matching receipt or tag.
2. A load's description does not match the material received.
3. A record identifies the commodity but not its origin.
4. A counterpart disputes the quantity or condition accepted.
5. Two institutions claim priority over a scarce imported material.
6. A seal or sealing is authentic but its use does not establish that the holder had authority for this particular transaction.

**Confidence:** Attested for long-distance connections and imported materials; inferred for many routes and intermediaries; reconstructed for individual transactions, agreements, and named merchant-like actors.

### G. Collective labor, construction, and public works

**Type:** Task-specific mobilization of people, materials, and provisions across institutional and social relationships; not a documented universal corvée system.

**Evidence and functions**
- Monumental construction demonstrates the ability to organize substantial labor and material resources.
- Administrative records classify human resources and record allocations; the exact meaning of individual labor categories and their relationship to specific projects require careful philological treatment.
- The archaic title list includes a sign sequence tentatively interpreted by Nissen as a head/leader associated with ERIN, possibly a workforce. This is not sufficient to establish later meanings or a universal conscription system.
- Avoid the claim that all adult citizens owed a fixed number of days or that the city had a known ward-based labor quota system.

**Authority and people**
- A specific project may have an institutional sponsor, responsible official, skilled workers, general laborers, suppliers, and people responsible for provisions.
- The precise authority and obligations must be authored per project; do not infer them from a broad professional orientation or household background.

**Resources and flows**
- Clay, timber, stone, reeds, tools, transport, provisions, and task-specific labor.
- Construction materials and labor can be tracked without asserting a precise historical wage or ration schedule.

**Records and evidence**
- Material inventories, allocations, delivery accounts, workforce categories, and records of provisions.
- A project plan or schedule is a gameplay reconstruction unless tied to a specific attested source.

**Orientation relevance**
- **Civic:** high.
- **Religious:** high where the project is within a sacred/monumental precinct.
- **Commercial:** medium, through materials procurement and transport.
- **Martial:** medium/conditional, where a defensive or security-related task is specifically authored. This does not prove that construction was military-controlled.

**Playable administrative problems**
1. A work party lacks a material allocation.
2. A delivery is short or late, delaying a task.
3. Two tasks require the same scarce transport or material.
4. A workforce record does not match the provision issue.
5. Responsibility for a damaged structure or missing tool is disputed.
6. A task is described as complete, but the material evidence indicates unfinished work.

**Confidence:** Attested for monumental construction and administrative resource recording; inferred for large-scale labor coordination; reconstructed for specific labor obligations, project offices, and schedules.

### H. Security, coercion, and armed activity

**Type:** A historically present but poorly documented dimension of power and conflict; not an established standalone military bureaucracy.

**Evidence and functions**
- Late Uruk cylinder-seal imagery includes scenes interpreted as domination, captives, and violence. Interpretation of individual scenes and figures is not always certain.
- A recent study of Mesopotamian military iconography notes that the Uruk period is comparatively sparse in symbolic military evidence; the evidence includes a small number of seal impressions, and the meaning of the ruler/armed figure remains debated.
- Monumental architecture and interregional competition demonstrate organized power, but do not by themselves prove a standing army, conscription, standard equipment, or military scribes.
- The evidence does not establish muster rolls, city-ward quotas, fitness screening, a 500-person expedition, a formal quartermaster office, or a standard prisoner-to-workshop pipeline for c. 3200 BCE.

**Authority and people**
- A specific scenario may involve an authority figure, armed retainer, guard, carrier, institutional official, or person responsible for a sealed space.
- Do not create a named “military department” unless future research supports it.
- Do not assume every security action was performed by a professional soldier.

**Resources and flows**
- Access to storerooms, containers, transport, provisions, and valuable materials may create security problems.
- Weapon inventories, military rations, prisoners, and booty must not be treated as routine administrative categories without specific evidence.

**Records and evidence**
- Seals and sealings can establish access, custody, or responsibility in context.
- Commodity and transport records may be relevant to a security event, but are not automatically military records.
- Iconographic evidence should be presented as imagery, not as a complete description of actual force organization.

**Orientation relevance**
- **Martial:** provisional and low-confidence. A player can be oriented toward security, protection, transport, and mobilization matters where authored, but should not begin as a quartermaster in a documented standing army.
- **Civic:** medium, through institutional authority and protection of resources.
- **Religious:** conditional, where sacred property or precinct security is involved.
- **Commercial:** conditional, where the security of a delivery or route is at issue.

**Playable administrative problems**
1. A sealed storeroom has been opened without a clear record of authorization.
2. A delivery is delayed or diverted, and the evidence does not distinguish theft, emergency, or an authorized change.
3. A valuable object has been moved, but the custody trail is incomplete.
4. An official claims authority to release goods, but the relevant authorization is unclear.
5. A group assigned to a task has not returned with all equipment or materials.

These are security and accountability scenarios, not proof of a Uruk military office. Any scenario involving armed conflict, captives, or an expedition requires a separate historical review.

**Confidence:** Attested for limited violent imagery and seal use; inferred for the existence of organized security arrangements; reconstructed for nearly all detailed military-administrative workflows.

## 4. Cross-path access and responsibility matrix

The following table describes likely *contact*, not exclusive membership or guaranteed access.

| Organizational context | Civic | Religious | Commercial | Martial |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Eanna administration | Core contact | Core contact | Procurement/delivery contact | Conditional security or mobilization contact |
| Anu/Kullaba precinct | Shared construction/resource matters | Core sacred-context contact | Material/delivery contact | No default role |
| Institutional households/work groups | Coordination/accountability | Personnel attached to cultic activity | Counterparty/transport contact | Only if a specific task calls for it |
| Agricultural/pastoral supply | Allocation and storage | Offerings/provisioning context | Movement and exchange | Conditional travel/provision context |
| Craft/processing | Institutional issue/output accounts | Sacred goods/ceremonial production | Materials and finished goods | Specific equipment request only |
| Exchange/procurement networks | Institutional procurement | Goods for precinct use | Core contact | Conditional route/security context |
| Collective works | Core coordination contact | Monumental precinct works | Supply and transport | Conditional defensive/security works |
| Security/armed activity | Institutional authority | Protection of precinct/property | Delivery/route protection | Provisional orientation, not a documented corps |

An orientation shapes the player's initial people, responsibilities, and likely opportunities. It does not create an entitlement to enter every institution or perform every action. Actual access is governed by relationships, circumstances, appointments, skill, and the authority represented in the shared institutional model.

## 5. What this means for the four starting orientations

### Civic

**Starting fantasy:** You are learning to make institutional work legible and accountable: goods, people, stores, allocations, and obligations must agree.

**Best Era I contexts:** Eanna administrative activity; provisioning networks; institutional work groups; collective projects.

**Avoid:** a modern civil-service department, secular state bureaucracy, or universal centralized command.

### Religious

**Starting fantasy:** Your work is connected to the material and administrative responsibilities of a sacred precinct and its activities.

**Best Era I contexts:** Eanna's overlapping ceremonial and administrative activity; Anu/Kullaba as a sacred/monumental setting with thinner direct administrative evidence; goods or tasks whose purpose is cultic or ceremonial.

**Avoid:** a separate church hierarchy outside economic life; a fully documented daily divine household schedule; assuming Eanna and Anu have distinct, fully known bureaucracies.

### Commercial

**Starting fantasy:** Your work connects goods, people, and places; the difficulty is knowing what was sent, what arrived, who accepted it, and what the record actually proves.

**Best Era I contexts:** procurement, transport, local and interregional exchange, material provenance, delivery/custody, institutional counterparties.

**Avoid:** importing later private merchant houses, modern contracts, or a universal market economy.

### Martial

**Starting fantasy:** Your work concerns the protection and movement of people and valuable goods, and the accountability of tasks undertaken under conditions of risk.

**Best Era I contexts:** specific security matters, sealed spaces, transport risk, emergency movement, and mobilization scenarios only where the scenario's authority and evidence are authored.

**Avoid:** professional standing army, quartermaster corps, city-ward conscription, standardized military rations, Bronze Age equipment sets, routine booty/captive accounts.

**Recommendation:** Retain Martial as a player-facing orientation. Its Era I expression remains provisional and low-confidence; do not make it mechanically symmetrical with better-attested contexts. Its opening may be a limited institutional responsibility or personal connection, not a formal military posting. Use “Security and Mobilization” as the local Era I gloss.

## 7. Implications for character creation

The previous Uruk character-creation matrix should be revised after this institutional model is approved. Specific changes recommended:

1. **Household origins remain broad life-history contexts**, not four fixed economic classes. Add the possibility that a character's household has ties to an institutional household or work group only after the exact relation is authored and historically reviewed.
2. **Scribal entry routes should connect to a real institutional responsibility**, such as provisioning, commodity movement, a precinct activity, a production account, or a delivery chain. Avoid implying that a formal school is documented.
3. **Civic and Religious orientation should not determine different institutions by default.** They should differ through the player's initial responsibilities, contacts, and interpretation of the same overlapping institutional world.
4. **Commercial orientation should not imply independent private trading status.** It can begin through a relationship to transport, procurement, delivery, or an external counterparty.
5. **Martial orientation should not grant combat skill or a military appointment.** It should begin through a defensible contact or task context, with explicit low-confidence framing until more research is complete.
6. **Familiarity should be commodity-, process-, and context-specific.** Examples include grain storage, livestock accounting, clay/tablet handling, sealing/custody, transport/delivery, or a particular craft material.
7. **The Storehouse Reckoning remains unchanged.** The tutorial is a fictional supervised exercise. Its evidence and assessment are not made dependent on the character's path; familiarity can shape interpretation only.

- **§4 Era identity:** Strengthens the administrative and material identity of Era I.
- **§7 Storehouse Reckoning:** Supports early record, commodity, numerical, and sealing evidence without altering the tutorial.
- **§15.3 Institutions and factions:** Directly applies the shared institutional model, era-specific rosters, overlapping contacts, and non-exclusive access rules.
- **§15.4–15.5 Social network and NPCs:** Gives people, responsibilities, relationships, and commitments specific institutional contexts.
- **§19 Content and scenario quality:** Supplies source confidence and scenario seed principles.
- **§23 Open Design Work:** Advances item 13 (era-specific institutional rosters and content), item 22 (starting circumstances and orientation benefits), and item 25 (situated interpretation).

No locked invariant is changed. No new progression track, resource balance, AP rule, activity layer, or generative-AI dependency is introduced.

## 9. Cross-department impact

- **Game Design:** Build opportunities from specific institutional matters and responsibilities. Professional orientation shapes starting context, not exclusive action access. Use administrative discrepancies and custody problems as the core conflict.
- **Historical Research:** Highest-priority follow-up is the philology of archaic titles and personnel categories; Anu/Kullaba dedication and function; the range of Eanna activities; degree of control over craft production; and evidence for organized violence/security.
- **Engineering & Architecture:** Model institutions as shared entities with responsibilities, authority, resources, personnel, relationships, and active matters. Keep orientation, affiliation, appointment, qualification, and relationship separate. Do not create a new institutional subsystem for each orientation.
- **Art & UI/UX:** Eanna should be a complex multi-use precinct, not simply a temple façade or factory. Anu/Kullaba should feel monumental and sacred, but should not be dressed as a later ziggurat complex without date-specific support. Visualize tablets, tags, sealings, containers, stores, workshops, and material flows.
- **Audio:** Establish work through clay, stylus, seals, vessels, animals, transport, building, and crowd ambience. Do not presume battlefield audio as a core Martial-orientation signature.
- **Production & QA:** Create a historical claim ledger. Each content asset and scenario should have a period, source, confidence, and list of assumptions. QA must test path overlap, non-exclusive access, and identical objective evidence in the tutorial.

## 10. Decisions for Game Director review

The design work supports the following recommendations but does not approve them:

1. **Institutional model:** Adopt overlapping institutional contexts rather than a four-department map.
2. **Eanna:** Make it the principal Era I administrative setting, with multiple functions and uncertain internal divisions.
3. **Anu/Kullaba:** Include as a distinct monumental/sacred precinct, but do not invent a parallel administrative archive or claim a certain divine dedication.
4. **Civic/Religious:** Preserve as separate starting orientations whose contexts overlap heavily.
5. **Commercial:** Focus on procurement, exchange, transport, and counterparties rather than a modern merchant institution.
6. **Martial:** Retain as the player-facing orientation, with lower historical confidence in Era I and no invented standing army or military scribal corps. Use “Security and Mobilization” as the local Era I gloss.
7. **Labor:** Treat collective labor as a task-specific relationship among institutions and people; do not assume universal corvée rules or city-ward quotas.
8. **Character creation:** Revise the prior matrix against the approved institutional model and Martial framing.

## 11. Research basis

1. Hans J. Nissen, “Uruk: Early Administration Practices and the Development of Proto-Cuneiform Writing,” *Archéo-Nil* 26 (2016), pp. 33–48. https://www.persee.fr/doc/arnil_1161-0492_2016_num_26_1_1102  
   - Primary basis for Uruk IV administrative evidence, Eanna, dating limitations, warehouses, commodity flows, title lists, information technologies, seals, and the speculative nature of early training institutions.
2. Giacomo Benati, “The Construction of Large-scale Networks in Late Chalcolithic Mesopotamia: Emergent Political Institutions and Their Strategies,” in *Urbanized Landscapes in Early Syro-Mesopotamia and Prehispanic Mesoamerica* (2018), pp. 103–122. https://site.unibo.it/eduu/en/work-package-1-archaeological-cultural-and-environmental-research/results/978-3-447-11086-0_free-open-access_.pdf  
   - Basis for multiple Eanna functions, the administrative corpus, food production/storage/distribution, sealing, and cautions about archaeological context and institutional control.
3. Vitali Bartash, “Age, Gender and Labor: Recording Human Resources in 3350–2500 BC Mesopotamia,” in Agnès Garcia-Ventura (ed.), *What’s in a Name?* (AOAT 440, 2018), pp. 45–80. https://www.assyriologie.uni-muenchen.de/personen/ehemalige_ma/bartash/publ_bartash/bartash_2018_aoat_440.pdf  
   - Basis for recorded human-resource categories, personnel attached to central institutions, dependents/workers, and food allocations. The 3350–2500 date range requires care when attributing details to c. 3200 specifically.
4. Gil J. Stein, “The Political Economy of Mesopotamian Colonial Encounters,” in *The Archaeology of Colonial Encounters* (2005), pp. 143–172. https://www.academia.edu/415177/2005_Gil_J_Stein_The_Political_Economy_of_Mesopotamian_Colonial_Encounters_Pp_143_172_in_G_Stein_ed_The_Archaeology_of_Colonial_Encounters_Santa_Fe_SAR_Press  
   - Basis for raw-material dependencies, interregional networks, and caution about centralized political control.
5. “The Uruk Phenomenon,” in Karen Radner, Nadine Moeller, and D. T. Potts (eds.), *The Oxford History of the Ancient Near East*, vol. 1 (2020). https://www.academia.edu/52071970/2020_The_Uruk_Phenomenon  
   - Basis for the difference between Eanna's tablet corpus and Kullaba/White Temple evidence; the latter lacks a comparable proto-cuneiform archive, and the temple's dedication is uncertain.
6. “Mesopotamia and the ‘New’ Military History,” Oriental Institute, University of Chicago. https://oi.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/new_military_history_2011.pdf  
   - Basis for caution about the scarcity of military iconography in Uruk-period Mesopotamia and the limited nature of the evidence.
7. “Soldiers and Prisoners in Motion in Mesopotamian Iconography during the Early Bronze Age,” *Arts* 13:4 (2024). https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0752/13/4/132  
   - Basis for the interpretation and dating of late fourth-millennium imagery of armed figures and captives, while emphasizing uncertainty about ruler identity and political organization.
8. Digital Corpus of Cuneiform Lexical Texts, “The Archaic Lexical Corpus.” https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/dcclt/intro/arch_intro.html  
   - Basis for early lexical lists as teaching/reference tools and their relationship to the administrative writing system.
9. Digital Corpus of Cuneiform Lexical Texts, “Introduction: What is a Lexical List?” https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/dcclt/intro/lexical_intro.html  
   - Basis for the earliest thematic lists of professions, vessels, trees, animals, and other categories.

**Research limitation:** This is a focused design synthesis, not a comprehensive institutional history of Uruk. The administrative corpus is unevenly preserved and interpreted. The matrix deliberately distinguishes evidence for activities and materials from the much weaker evidence for exact office structures, jurisdiction, legal status, and individual career routes.
