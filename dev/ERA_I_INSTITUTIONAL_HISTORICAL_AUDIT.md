# Era I Institutional and Administrative Reality — Historical Audit

**Status:** Research/design assessment for Game Director review; not approved GDD canon  
**Date:** 2026-11-23  
**Scope:** Review of supplied Era I historical framing for Uruk IV–III / Late Uruk–Jemdet Nasr, c. 3200 BCE  
**Authority:** `docs/GDD.md` remains canonical. This note proposes no GDD revision and changes no production code.

## Executive finding

The supplied material identifies the right design center: Era I should feel like an administrative world in which institutions organize goods, labor, storage, production, exchange, and access. Civic and cultic activity should not be divided using modern secular-state versus organized-religion assumptions. Martial-oriented play, if retained, should focus on mobilization, security, logistics, and institutional accountability rather than a later professional army.

However, the supplied account is too certain about several disputed or unsupported reconstructions. It should not be adopted verbatim. In particular:
- Late Uruk political authority cannot safely be reduced to one unified “temple-state.”
- Eanna is a major and exceptionally well-documented administrative/cultic precinct, but a simple Eanna civic/economic versus Anu ritual division is not established.
- The interpretation of EN and the Warka Vase figure as a “priest-king” is disputed.
- Muster rolls, ward quotas, a conscript citizen army, a 500-person expedition, a standing armory, and a military scribe's standard duties are not demonstrated by the evidence reviewed.
- The four named titles do not constitute a documented Uruk IV–III career ladder.
- Proto-cuneiform is not accurately described as “1,200 pictographic signs” or as having no intellectual use beyond transaction data.
- Commodity-dependent numerical systems are real and valuable for gameplay, but the examples in the supplied material are oversimplified.
- Cylinder seals did more than act as signatures: sealings controlled access to doors and containers, identified institutional actors or processes, and participated in a wider accounting system.

## 1. Chronology and evidentiary limits

The game's c. 3200 BCE setting sits around the Late Uruk / Uruk IV–III transition. Published chronologies vary. A useful working approximation is Uruk IV around 3350–3200 BCE and Uruk III / Jemdet Nasr around 3200–3000 BCE; these dates should be treated as approximate rather than exact boundaries.

The archaeological record is uneven. Nissen emphasizes that Uruk IVa is far better excavated than earlier levels and that even the IVa tablets were often recovered in secondary rubbish deposits rather than secure contexts in which they were written or used. This limits fine-grained claims about individual offices, buildings, and administrative routines.

**Design consequence:** Era I content should distinguish:
- **Attested:** directly represented by securely dated material or texts.
- **Inferred:** plausible interpretation of multiple lines of evidence.
- **Reconstruction:** a playable scenario built to make a human-scale world from incomplete evidence.

The game should not turn a plausible reconstruction into a confident historical assertion through an authoritative tooltip or glossary.

## 2. Political and institutional landscape

### 2.1 What is supportable

Uruk was a very large, increasingly complex urban center. The Late Uruk archaeological record shows monumental precincts and extensive administrative technologies. Eanna is a major focus of excavated Late Uruk administrative material: tablets, sealings, and other information-storage devices. The archaic corpus from Uruk includes thousands of texts, predominantly administrative accounts involving commodities, land, production, storage, distribution, and labor.

Eanna's archaeological assemblage and architecture support multiple activities: administration, storage/distribution, large-scale ceremonial or cultic gatherings, and possibly craft production. But the deposits and excavation history do not permit every building or room to be assigned a precise function.

### 2.2 What should not be simplified

“Temple-state” is useful as a warning against modern church/state separation, but it becomes misleading if it implies one fully unified bureaucracy or a single uncontested center of power. Scholarship has emphasized uncertainty about how centralized Uruk's institutions were. Some accounts treat temples as powerful institutions; others describe a multicentric or incompletely centralized political economy, with rulers and temple institutions both important.

Likewise, the evidence does not support a rigid map in which:
- Eanna = civic/economic government, and
- Anu/Kullaba = separate ritual administration.

Both districts matter to Uruk's sacred and monumental landscape. Their exact administrative division at the game's opening date is not established by the reviewed evidence. Avoid calling the Anu complex a “ziggurat” as though its later architectural form and institutional role were already present in c. 3200 BCE. Use **Anu/Kullaba precinct** or **Anu district** unless a specific structure and date are verified.

### 2.3 EN and the Warka Vase

EN is a prominent sign/term in the Uruk IV–III corpus. The Warka Vase depicts a high-status figure in a ritual/offerings scene. Connecting the figure with an EN ruler is a scholarly interpretation, but the label “priest-king” is contested. One line of critique argues that the priestly interpretation projects later meanings of the term backward and that the visual identification itself is uncertain.

**Design consequence:** portray a high-status authority figure whose role can involve ritual, resource management, and political power, but do not make “priest-king” a settled title or assume a fully documented constitutional office. If EN appears in a glossary or dialogue, identify the reading and interpretation as provisional.

## 3. Institutional gameplay model for Era I

Retain the GDD's four professional orientations as starting choices. Do not convert them into four exclusive institutions or modern departments.

| GDD path | Era I framing | Strongest support | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Civic | Institutional administration: land, labor, production, storage, redistribution, and public/collective works | Administrative texts and monumental institutional settings | Do not imply a modern secular civil service or one fully centralized state. |
| Religious | Cultic administration: offerings, ritual goods, ceremonial provisioning, sacred property, and the people responsible for maintaining cultic activity | Monumental cultic precincts, offering imagery, and the close relationship between institutions and divine service | This is not a separate “church” outside economic life. Cultic and economic responsibilities overlap. |
| Commercial | Exchange, transport, resource procurement, deliveries, and relationships across local and long-distance networks | Uruk-period exchange networks and evidence for movement of raw materials and commodities | Do not assume later private merchant houses, modern markets, or a fully documented merchant profession in the later sense. |
| Martial | Security, mobilization, organized labor, transport, provisioning, and accountability connected to armed activity or defensive works | Conflict imagery, interregional competition, and broader evidence for organized labor and resource movement | Do not assert a standing army, standard conscription system, military scribal corps, or detailed muster structure without new evidence. |

**Important:** Civic and Religious should be overlapping orientations. A player choosing either may encounter the same precinct, administrators, labor systems, stores, and records from a different responsibility or relationship. They are not “state employee” versus “priest” in a modern sense.

**Civic infrastructure and corvée-like labor** should be cross-institutional content, not a fifth path. Workforces, resource allocation, construction, maintenance, and seasonal labor can appear in multiple institutional contexts.

## 4. Martial orientation: logistics without invented army bureaucracy

The supplied proposal is right to avoid combat-RPG framing and later professional armies. It goes too far when it specifies citizen conscription by city ward, muster rolls, fitness screening, evasion tracking, a 500-person expedition to Susa, standardized armory accounts, and a fixed pipeline from captives to textile workshops.

The reviewed evidence does not establish those as Uruk IV–III practices. The absence of evidence for a professional standing army is not proof that no organized military force existed; it is a reason not to author a highly specific force structure as fact.

### Recommended Era I interpretation

Keep **Martial** as the GDD's initial orientation, but frame its first playable expression as **security and mobilization administration**:
- tracking people assigned to a specific collective task, where the scenario provides an authored basis;
- organizing transport animals, loads, routes, and delivery responsibilities;
- accounting for food and other provisions for a work party or traveling group;
- recording the receipt, custody, and return of equipment or materials;
- managing a defensive-work or emergency labor obligation;
- handling disputed responsibility, missing goods, unauthorized access, or incomplete returns.

These are plausible administrative problem types, not claims that a standardized Uruk military office performed all of them. The scenario should state who ordered the work, what institution or authority is involved, and what evidence exists.

Do not use:
- “Quartermaster,” “Muster-Master,” or “Expeditionary Commissary” as historical job titles;
- fixed ward quotas or conscription rules;
- a standard 500-person army;
- a universal military ration table;
- a guaranteed captive-to-workshop pipeline;
- Bronze Age or later military equipment presented as standard c. 3200 BCE kit.

A Martial-oriented apprentice can begin with a relationship to a person involved in security, transport, labor coordination, or institutional provisioning. The character does not need to be a soldier, and the path should not grant combat skills.

### Defensive walls

The famous Uruk city wall should not be casually placed at the opening date. UNESCO's Uruk chronology places the large double mudbrick wall in the first half of the third millennium BCE, after the Late Uruk setting. If a wall-construction scenario is used in Era I, research and date the particular structure or activity first; do not use the later wall as proof of an already-established 3200 BCE military system.

## 5. Titles and professional progression

The proposed ladder is not historically safe:

| Supplied title | Evidence assessment | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| **Sanga-bisaĝ** — “Junior Archivist / Reed-Cutter” | No reliable evidence reviewed for this compound title or translation. The archaic SANGA sign/term has a debated reading and function; one proposal connects it with *umbisaĝ* (“scribe/administrator”), while the later title *sanga* as chief temple administrator is securely attested from Early Dynastic contexts. | Do not use as the apprentice title. |
| **Agrig** — “Steward / Ration Distributor” | The lexical evidence surfaced for *agrig* begins in Early Dynastic IIIa/IIIb and later, not Uruk IV. | Do not use as a c. 3200 BCE career tier. |
| **Nubanda** — “Overseer / Inspector” | ORACC's ePSD2 lists attestations beginning in Early Dynastic I–II / IIIa, with the large majority much later. It is not a secure Uruk IV title. | Do not use as a Uruk IV tier. |
| **Sanga** — “Chief Temple Administrator” | The later title is attested beginning in the Early Dynastic period. The archaic SANGA sign occurs in Uruk material, but its reading and office should not simply be equated with the later title. | Use only with explicit archaic-sign/reading caveat, not as the final rung of a documented ladder. |

There is no evidence for these four as sequential levels of one career. The GDD's Professional Rank is a game abstraction derived from skills; it should not be mapped onto invented ancient job titles.

**Recommended progression language for Era I:** use descriptive English functional labels in the UI—apprentice, trusted assistant, responsible clerk/administrator, senior administrator—only as gameplay descriptions, not translations of attested Uruk titles. Where an actual ancient term is used, show its date range, source, and uncertainty in the historical reference layer.

## 6. Proto-cuneiform and numerical systems

### 6.1 Writing's initial purpose

The strongest framing is that the earliest writing system emerged primarily to support economic administration and did not initially aim to transcribe spoken language. The language behind the earliest texts remains uncertain; identifying it as Sumerian is probable in some scholarship but not proven.

However, “strictly administrative” and “no intellectual use” are too absolute. Archaic lexical lists—organized lists of titles, professions, cities, animals, and other categories—are also part of the earliest written corpus. They likely served as reference and teaching tools and show that the new technology was used to classify and organize knowledge, not only to record individual transactions.

**Gameplay consequence:** do not put ordinary spoken dialogue, letters, speeches, poetry, or narrative prose onto Uruk IV tablets. But allow spoken dialogue and social negotiation in the game. The player can discuss, question, persuade, and interpret orally while the document itself remains a structured administrative record.

### 6.2 Sign counts

The claim of “about 1,200 pictographic signs” should not be used. Published counts vary with period, sign-list conventions, variants, and whether numerical signs are included. Nissen's 2016 discussion describes a system of close to 600 signs for the level IV material; other sign inventories count more, and the signs are not all pictographs. Many are abstract, conventional, or derived from combinations and differentiations of existing marks.

**Gameplay consequence:** teach a limited, authored working repertoire relevant to the current task, not an arbitrary claim that the apprentice learns all 1,200 signs.

### 6.3 Numerical systems

This is one of the strongest and most distinctive mechanics available for Era I. Proto-cuneiform used multiple numerical/metrological systems. The system selected depended on what was being counted or measured: discrete objects, different rationed products, area, weight, time, and capacity/volume. Some numerical signs can have different values within different systems, but this is not adequately represented by the simplified examples “10 for sheep, 6 for grain area, 18 for beer.”

**Recommended rule for gameplay:**

> The player must identify the commodity or quantity type and the relevant administrative context before choosing the numerical system. A numerically plausible entry can still be wrong if it uses the wrong metrological system.

This creates authentic administrative judgment:
- What is being counted?
- Is it a discrete object, a ration, an area, a weight, a duration, or a capacity?
- Which system is appropriate?
- Does the tablet's heading/context make that choice clear?
- Are two records using comparable units before they are reconciled?

Do not reduce the system to a single base-60 arithmetic puzzle. The challenge is contextual metrology and correct interpretation, not just arithmetic.

## 7. Rations and beveled-rim bowls

Administrative texts document distributions and production involving grain, cereal products, beer, and other commodities. Beveled-rim bowls are often interpreted as ration containers because of their quantity, standardization, expendability, and relationship to ration signs. Their exact use and contents remain debated; proposals include grain, prepared food, or liquid/semi-liquid products.

Therefore:
- Barley and beer can be relevant to Era I provisioning.
- Beveled-rim bowls can be used as visual evidence or a material clue.
- Do not state that all such bowls held a standard barley/beer ration.
- Do not make them proof of a military ration system.
- Keep the GDD's two player balances—Money and Provisions. Beer can be an in-world commodity or record category, never a third player balance.

## 8. Seals, custody, and accountability

Cylinder seals and clay sealings are excellent Era I gameplay material, but “signature” is incomplete. Sealings could secure doors, containers, and packages; their reverse impressions can preserve the shape of the object or closure. Seal imagery could identify an institution, production unit, sender, controlling authority, commodity, or administrative process. They participated in an integrated system with counters, clay envelopes, tablets, and written records.

**Recommended gameplay opportunities:**
- a storeroom closure whose sealing is broken or replaced;
- a container whose sealing does not match the expected sender or responsible party;
- a delivery where the tablet, commodity, and sealing tell different stories;
- a disputed claim about who had custody or authority to open a space;
- an impression that is genuine but used outside its proper scope;
- an incomplete or damaged sealing that leaves the evidence inconclusive.

Do not reduce every seal to a personal signature or assume that every official had a unique personal seal in the modern sense. The meaning of an impression must be established from the particular administrative context.

## 9. Revised Era I design principles

1. **Institutions overlap.** Cultic, economic, political, and administrative functions are intertwined; do not create a modern church/state split.
2. **Authority is situated.** Identify the people, institutions, precincts, and specific responsibilities behind each record; do not assume a single all-powerful central bureaucracy.
3. **The Martial orientation is administrative.** Focus on security, mobilization, transport, provisioning, and accountability; do not invent a professional standing army or fixed conscription system.
4. **Do not backdate titles.** Use descriptive UI labels unless the ancient term is attested for the specific period and context.
5. **Writing is structured and constrained.** Early tablets record administrative information and lexical categories, not ordinary prose; oral conversation remains available to the player.
6. **Metrology is contextual.** Correct numerical notation depends on the category and system, not merely arithmetic.
7. **Seals are custody and authority evidence.** They authenticate and control access in context; they are not simply signatures.
8. **Material culture is evidence, not a universal answer key.** Bowls, seals, tablets, counters, and tools should require contextual interpretation.
9. **Historical uncertainty is visible.** Mark reconstructions as reconstructions in the design bible and historical reference layer.

## 10. GDD alignment and cross-track impact

### GDD alignment

- **§3.4 Professional orientations:** preserves Civic, Religious, Commercial, Martial as initial orientations, while making their Uruk expression overlap and historically situated.
- **§4.1–4.2 Era identity:** strengthens Era I as early administrative recordkeeping and emergence of writing.
- **§7 Storehouse Reckoning:** supports commodity accounting, contextual numerical systems, seal/custody evidence, and administrative judgment without changing the tutorial's locked structure.
- **§15 People and Institutions:** requires institutions to be modeled as overlapping organizations with specific authority and responsibility, not mutually exclusive modern departments.
- **§19 Content and Scenario Quality:** calls for source confidence labels and period-specific limits on records and terminology.
- **§23 Open Design Work:** informs Era I institution roster, career-path content, historical references, character creation, and situated knowledge.

No locked invariant is changed. No new resource balance is proposed. No change is made to the six-AP day, activity layers, progression tracks, or zero-runtime-generative-AI boundary.

### Cross-department impact

- **Game Design:** institutional jurisdiction and responsibility should drive opportunity and scenario differences; paths remain non-exclusive.
- **Historical Research:** targeted work remains on EN, Eanna/Anu functions, military organization, labor terms, titles, ration systems, and dating.
- **Engineering:** represent institutional affiliation, authority, custody, records, and commitments separately; do not use professional orientation as a substitute for any of them.
- **Art/UI:** emphasize clay tablets, numerical impressions, sealings, containers, storerooms, workshops, and precinct spaces; avoid later ziggurat/army iconography without period evidence.
- **Audio:** material sounds of clay, stylus, seals, vessels, workshops, and movement reinforce the era; avoid cinematic battlefield assumptions.
- **Production/QA:** maintain a claim ledger with source, period, confidence, and affected content; test that paths do not create unsupported exclusive institutions.

## 11. Recommended next design action

Before revising the character-creation matrix, build an **Era I Institution and Responsibility Matrix** with:
- the institution/precinct or organizational context;
- its attested or inferred functions;
- the people and authority relationships involved;
- the kinds of goods, labor, and records associated with it;
- which of the four player orientations might encounter it;
- what is known, inferred, or reconstructed;
- which playable administrative problems can be supported.

This should determine how Civic and Religious overlap, what a defensible Martial opening looks like, and how Commercial contacts fit the same world. Then revise the character-creation choices and tutorial context against that matrix.
