## Executive finding

The available scholarship supports a Late Uruk world with substantial institutional administration, movement and custody of goods, seals and sealings, organized labor, and complex urban organization. It does **not** support importing later Mesopotamian military institutions into Uruk IV as established fact. The evidence for identifiable warfare imagery is limited and debated; the archaic administrative corpus is overwhelmingly concerned with economic and institutional activity.

Accordingly, the four Martial families may be implemented as **provisional security, movement, mobilization, and custody situations** in the shared Assignment pool. They must not be represented as evidence for a standing army, formal guard corps, standardized military chain of command, conscription, muster rolls, quartermaster office, military scribal corps, standard equipment, or standard rations.

## Evidence and confidence

### 1. Administrative records and institutional responsibility

Hans J. Nissen's survey of Uruk's early administration describes the growth of administrative information systems, including seals, counters, and early writing. For Uruk IVa, the excavated evidence is concentrated in Eanna and provides insight into economic administration; the source base is uneven and does not reveal all aspects of society. Nissen discusses administrative supervision and information storage, not a documented military department.

Sallaberger and Schrakamp's overview of archaic texts reports that the corpus is dominated by administrative accounts. The Uruk material records economic aspects of large households, including land, harvest, storage, distribution, and the organization of work forces. This supports task-specific accountability for people, goods, and work as a cautious reconstruction; it does not establish the specific party, sponsor, or office in the approved Martial families.

**Implementation consequence:** use source records, inventories, dispatch/return accounts, testimony, and event-linked custody facts as the evidentiary spine. Keep each authored incident explicitly provisional. Do not claim that the underlying administrative record is an attested Uruk IV military form.

### 2. Seals, access, and custody

Late fourth-millennium seals and sealings are part of the administrative technology used to identify and control movement of goods. Research on the relationship between seals and early writing emphasizes the documentation of textile and vessel movement within and between settlements.

**Implementation consequence:** a seal impression can establish that a particular seal was impressed or that a sealing is present and can be compared with another impression. It cannot, by itself, prove the holder's authority for a particular opening, identify the person who physically entered, prove theft, or establish the complete custody chain. This directly supports the evidence limits in *The Door That Was Opened* and *The Trail of a Valued Object*.

No source located in this pass establishes a formal Uruk IV office of “door-warden.” The character Nin-dara must therefore be described as a household member entrusted with access to a door/space in this fictional occurrence, not as the holder of an attested office.

### 3. Warfare and military organization

The scholarly discussion of early Mesopotamian military history cautions that Uruk-period Mesopotamia (approximately 3400–3000 BCE) is comparatively poor in symbolic evidence for military power. The earliest identifiable warfare images in Mesopotamia are discussed as appearing at the end of the fourth millennium on cylinder-seal impressions from Uruk, but interpretation of ruler figures and the political meaning of such images remains debated.

The archaic text corpus does not provide a secure basis for reconstructing a standing army or standardized military administration in Uruk IV. Better documented military institutions and detailed army accounts belong to substantially later periods and must not be back-projected.

**Implementation consequence:** the Martial orientation is a gameplay lens for security, movement, mobilization, and accountability—not a claim that the character belongs to a formal army or military scribal corps. Avoid “soldier,” “guard captain,” “muster,” “escort corps,” “quartermaster,” “military ration,” and similar institutionalized language unless a later research pass establishes a specific, period-appropriate use. A task-specific group may be gathered for a defined work or movement episode, but it is not a standing unit.
