# People, Institutions, and World Simulation Specification

**Authority:** Supporting specification under [the canonical GDD](../GDD.md).  
**Status:** Consolidated from the approved GDD draft on 23 November 2026.

Era-specific historical claims and authored rosters belong in the relevant historical/content specifications.

## 15. People, Institutions, and Social Network

### 15.1 NPC agency, roles, and individual relationships

NPCs are people with interests and lives of their own, not characters waiting for the player to activate them. Relevant NPCs can pursue objectives, meet obligations, respond to changing circumstances, and interact with other people without the player's involvement. Their actions may change relationships, institutional circumstances, ongoing work, opportunities, and the player's interests. The player has agency, but no veto over everyone else's decisions.

Agency should be proportionate to an NPC's importance to play. Important recurring characters may have long-term ambitions alongside immediate objectives; other relevant NPCs may need only immediate concerns and obligations. Long-term ambitions can be reinforced, delayed, redirected, or undermined by events and competing priorities. NPC goals can conflict with one another, including within the same person.

Independent action must remain causally understandable rather than arbitrary. A consequential decision should make sense in light of the NPC's motivations, circumstances, capabilities, knowledge, relationships, resources, and authority. NPCs should not routinely act on information they could not plausibly obtain or exercise powers they do not possess. The player may know an action is coming, discover it afterward, or remain unaware until later evidence or interaction brings it to light; the game should not grant advance warning merely because an event affects the player.

This is a game, not an attempt to simulate every person's routine. Preserve and present NPC developments when they meaningfully affect people, obligations, relationships, institutions, opportunities, or ongoing work. Routine activity can be summarized or left in the background when its detail has no meaningful consequence for play.

When defining an NPC's role, keep three things distinct:
- **Social identity:** who the person is, what they want, whom they know, and what matters to them.
- **Institutional position:** their affiliations, responsibilities, authority, and limitations.
- **Current involvement:** what they are doing in a particular situation and what they know about it.

Responsibility for an outcome does not automatically confer authority over every action that contributes to it. Likewise, authority does not prove that a person acted, and participation does not by itself establish fault. Duties, decision rights, practical capacity, knowledge, and accountability should be considered separately when they matter to a playable situation. Historical job titles and designations must be interpreted in context; an attested designation is not automatically a fully understood job description or a reason to create a permanent NPC type.

Named NPCs are important recurring characters. The game should track individual relationships and relevant history rather than relying only on broad faction scores. Relationships are directional: each person has their own perspective on the other, and those perspectives need not match. The player may trust someone who distrusts them, or respect someone who dislikes them.

A relationship can contain several qualities at once. Trust, respect, affection, resentment, rivalry, familiarity, and obligation are not mutually exclusive states and should not be collapsed into one shared friendship value. A merchant may dislike the player yet still fulfill a tithe or other obligation; a personal relationship can influence how someone approaches an obligation without automatically erasing or overriding it. Conversely, fulfilling an obligation does not prove affection, loyalty, or trust.

Relationships develop through significant shared events or through accumulated meaningful interactions. One consequential experience can substantially change a relationship; repeated meaningful contact can gradually establish familiarity, trust, respect, resentment, or other qualities. Routine exchanges do not all need permanent individual memories. Preserve the experiences that explain a consequential relationship or later decision.

### Conflicting commitments and obligations

NPCs may hold several commitments at once. A conflict arises when two or more recognized commitments cannot all be fulfilled under the NPC's present circumstances, given available time, resources, knowledge, authority, or practical capacity. Conflicting obligations should create meaningful situations and decisions, not a universal hidden loyalty contest or a separate obligation-management simulation.

Keep the basis of a commitment identifiable when it matters to play. It may arise from a recorded allocation or agreement, an instruction, an office responsibility, a personal undertaking, or an expectation grounded in an ongoing relationship. A demand or request is not automatically a valid obligation, and a preference or desire is not equivalent to a duty. The game should preserve distinctions between what someone expects, what someone claims is required, and what the available evidence establishes. A commitment may be disputed, misunderstood, conditional, or beyond the person's authority or capacity.

When apparent commitments conflict, an NPC should have room to pursue a resolution before making an irreversible choice where circumstances permit. Contextually appropriate responses may include seeking clarification or authorization, requesting more time, negotiating, delegating, obtaining assistance, substituting or dividing performance, or referring the matter to someone else. These are possible responses, not a mandatory sequence or a universal procedure. The NPC's available choices depend on the actual situation and historical context.

If the conflict remains unresolved, the NPC's response should be understandable in light of their motivations, authority, dependence on others, relationships, knowledge, exposure to consequences, discretion, resources, and alternatives. Do not reduce the decision to one hidden loyalty or relationship score. Personality may shape how an NPC responds, but should not replace the concrete circumstances or grant knowledge and authority they do not possess.

Fulfilling one commitment does not silently erase another. The outstanding expectation or disputed matter may continue and affect later interactions, relationships, institutional circumstances, or work. Consequences are not automatic penalties: they depend on what the affected parties know, what they can do, and how they interpret the failure or explanation. Failure to fulfill a commitment does not by itself establish dishonesty or bad faith.

Player-facing presentation should distinguish:
- **Observable action or result:** what happened or what can be directly observed, such as a delayed delivery, refusal, or recorded quantity.
- **Attributed explanation or claim:** what an NPC says happened, what instruction they report receiving, or what another person alleges.
- **Supporting evidence:** records, observations, testimony, or later confirmation that support, qualify, or contradict an explanation.

An NPC's explanation may be sincere and mistaken, incomplete, self-serving, or unsupported. It should not become established fact merely because the NPC states it. The player's knowledge remains situated; the player may encounter the action first, hear an explanation later, or obtain supporting evidence through people, records, and other plausible channels.

Historical evidence may establish that people participated in differentiated relationships to goods, labor, allocations, and accounts without revealing how they deliberated between competing demands. Where the historical record does not preserve motives, procedures, or individual decisions, those elements are designed reconstruction and must not be presented as recovered historical fact. The game should not invent an obligation merely to explain an NPC's obstruction; the commitment should have a basis in the situation and remain meaningful after the immediate encounter.

#### Factions, affiliations, and overlapping loyalties

Factions provide social and institutional context for behavior, but they do not replace individual relationships or reduce every person to a single group identity. A person may belong to, depend on, cooperate with, or be in conflict with more than one faction at the same time. Membership and affiliation are therefore not assumed to be mutually exclusive.

Faction affiliations can shape how NPCs interpret one another, cooperate, compete, share access, exchange information, or respond to disputes. Those effects are contextual: an individual's own relationships, commitments, authority, interests, knowledge, and circumstances may reinforce or contradict the expectations of a faction. Two members of the same faction may disagree; people from different factions may cooperate or maintain strong personal ties.

Faction standing is a useful broad representation of the player's or an institution's relationship with a group. It must not become a universal substitute for directional person-to-person relationships, nor a hidden score that automatically determines every NPC action. Where faction membership matters to a consequential decision, preserve the relevant affiliations and the specific reason they matter. Do not force every NPC into a faction merely to populate a social graph.

Relationship information can include:
- Trust, affinity, resentment, or professional respect.
- Prior assignments and shared events.
- Promises, debts, favors, and obligations.
- Mentorship and apprenticeship.
- Family and household connections.
- Professional collaboration or rivalry.
- Institutional role and changing authority.
- Whether the person is alive, active, retired, displaced, or represented by descendants or successors, where relevant to the era.

Not every relationship needs every field. The record should capture details that matter to future play.

### 15.2 Persistent social network

Characters, locations, institutions, and relationships are central to continuity. Contacts should recur where historically and narratively plausible. A person may introduce a new patron, recommend an apprentice, call in a favor, or become involved in a later dispute.

The social network should be persistent enough that the player recognizes consequences across years and generations. A new era should introduce new people and institutions without making the previous history feel erased.

### 15.3 Institutions and factions

Institutions use a **shared underlying gameplay model** rather than separate mechanical systems for each institutional or career type. Their common properties may include identity and historical context, responsibilities and authority, resources and personnel, relationships with other institutions and people, and current circumstances or unresolved matters. The model should remain proportionate: institutions have objectives, constraints, and organizational interests, but do not need the full personality, memory, or decision-making complexity of individual NPCs.

Unresolved institutional matters are durable, typed world records with stable IDs, an owning institution, a concise summary, lifecycle status, and relevant provenance, location, or scheduled development. Institutions reference active matters by ID; presentation text is not a state key. Legacy saves containing matter descriptions are normalized into canonical matter records. A bounded daily world tick advances only authored consequential matters and scheduled developments; it is not an autonomous simulation of every citizen.

Institutional rosters are specific to each historical era. Do not assume that the same organizations persist unchanged across eras or simply receive new names and visual skins. Each era should introduce institutions appropriate to its historical setting. The four initial professional orientations—Civic, Religious, Commercial, and Martial—can guide a small number of especially relevant institutions for each orientation, alongside general institutions and organizations that people from multiple orientations may encounter.

Professional orientations do not create exclusive access or membership boundaries. An orientation shapes a character's starting position, relationships, responsibilities, opportunities, and likely institutional contacts; it does not prevent them from interacting with institutions associated with other orientations.

Institutional differences should initially be expressed through historical and narrative character, style, terminology, customs, attitudes, priorities, and context. Differences in authority, responsibilities, resources, or available actions should be represented through the shared model's properties and circumstances, not bespoke mechanics. Introduce mechanically distinct institutional types only if later gameplay demonstrates a genuine need.

Institutional continuity across eras is not assumed. Professional traditions, records, knowledge, family or social connections, and other specific legacies may persist where the historical context supports them, but that does not require the institution itself to survive. Faction standing can represent broad relationships with groups such as temple authorities, citizens, guilds, merchants, or other era-appropriate organizations. It complements, but does not replace, individual NPC relationships.

### 15.4 Historical events

Historical events can alter institutions, locations, opportunities, and relationships. Events may create Challenges, change Work conditions, or reshape the social network. They should be represented through human consequences rather than as detached timeline announcements.

The daily world tick runs during END_DAY after opportunity expiry is settled. It advances explicitly scheduled, consequential developments for active institutional matters and records resulting events and follow-up matters. It remains bounded and deterministic; routine NPC activity without meaningful consequences stays outside the simulation.

### 15.5 Bounded NPC simulation and world advancement

The world must feel active beyond the player's immediate interactions without requiring a continuously simulated population. Simulate selectively: preserve the people, relationships, commitments, and institutional matters whose developments can produce meaningful consequences. Represent routine life in summaries or background context when individual detail would not change a later decision, relationship, obligation, opportunity, or event.

The existing `Person` model is the foundation for consequential NPCs. Extend it with relevant social identity, institutional position, current circumstances, commitments, and life-stage information rather than creating a disconnected parallel NPC simulation. Not every person needs every field or the same level of simulation. Use typed life stages where a person's generational or professional standing matters and exact age is unknown or unnecessary. Do not invent numerical ages to create a false impression of historical precision. An illustrative stage vocabulary is:

```ts
type LifeStage =
  | 'apprentice'
  | 'junior_scribe'
  | 'established_scribe'
  | 'active_official'
  | 'senior_scribe'
  | 'craft_practitioner'
  | 'elder';
```

Life stage is contextual information, not a substitute for a person's skills, authority, health, actual age where known, or current circumstances.

Keep **circumstances** and **commitments** distinct. A circumstance describes a person's current situation or constraint; a commitment records an undertaking or responsibility they have accepted or that is established by an identifiable basis. A request, demand, preference, ambition, or expectation does not automatically become a commitment. Preserve the source and scope of a commitment when it may affect later decisions. NPC ambitions and preferences should also remain distinguishable from obligations.

Consequential NPCs may pursue immediate objectives and longer-term ambitions independently of the player. Their goals can conflict with other NPCs' goals, with institutional priorities, and with their own commitments. Those conflicts can produce developments, losses, delays, alliances, disputes, or other consequences that do not involve the player. The player is not the center or automatic cause of every event. Independent action must still follow from plausible motivations, knowledge, relationships, authority, resources, and constraints; it must not be arbitrary or omniscient.

NPC development should be updated only when there is a reason to do so: an event changes their circumstances, a scheduled commitment or matter reaches a relevant point, a condition becomes true, or a player interaction requires a current decision. This permits event-driven, scheduled, and on-demand evaluation without ticking every NPC through a full daily decision loop. Track knowledge, memories, and relationship history at the level needed to explain consequential behavior; do not create a complete transcript of every routine interaction.

Player-facing dossiers and reports must respect the distinction between public circumstances, declared responsibilities, and private objectives. A dossier may show circumstances or commitments that are publicly observable or institutionally declared, but it must not expose a private objective merely because the simulation stores it. The player learns private motives through plausible testimony, records, observation, or interaction; the existence of an objective in world state does not grant the active character knowledge of it.

#### Institutional matters as durable world state

An institutional matter is a durable, typed record of an unresolved or developing situation, not merely a notification or a block of presentation text. A matter should have:
- A stable identifier and matter type.
- An owning institution.
- A concise summary and lifecycle status.
- Its creation day and relevant schedule or condition for further development.
- Involved people and relevant locations.
- Provenance: the event, record, instruction, agreement, or other source that establishes the matter.
- Authored development rules and possible consequences.

Institutions should reference active matters by stable identifier. Matter descriptions and display text are not state keys. Resolved matters may remain in historical records where their outcomes matter, while unresolved matters persist across Workdays and saves. Do not delete an unresolved matter merely because it did not produce a player-facing opportunity.

Completing an Assignment generated from a matter records the submitted report and its provenance against that same matter; it does not automatically resolve the underlying institutional situation. Report filing, institutional assessment, authorization, and execution are distinct events. A matter remains active or developed until an authored institutional response or world development changes its status. The same matter must not generate the same Assignment again after it has already produced an offer, regardless of whether that offer was completed, declined, lapsed, or cancelled.

The END_DAY world update is a bounded, deterministic transition over active consequential matters and explicitly scheduled developments. A development advances only when its authored date or condition is met. It records a deterministic event with a stable identifier, updates the affected matter and relevant institutional references, and may create or update follow-up matters. Unresolved matters remain active. The same starting state and day must produce the same world transition; random daily busywork and arbitrary event rolls are not substitutes for causality.

#### From world state to playable opportunities

World matters, NPC circumstances, and unmet needs can create potential opportunities, but they do not automatically become Assignments. An authored activity definition determines whether a situation is suitable as an interactive Assignment, optional Challenge, conversation, institutional decision, or another form of play. The opportunity-generation process should check the situation's provenance and relevance, the character's access and skill qualification, required authority, involved people's knowledge and willingness, current commitments, and whether an equivalent opportunity is already active or recently resolved.

**Current implementation boundary (Phase 3B):** the Assignment generator discovers opportunities by matching active institutional matters to authored Uruk Assignment opportunity definitions. It requires the matter to be active and still listed by its owning institution, carries the stable matter ID into the offer, and prevents another offer from the same matter. Authored definitions own the activity, context, compensation, and delivery explanation; the engine owns deterministic discovery and bounded presentation. This is the first matter-driven discovery step, not completion of the full eligibility model. Rank-based reach, access, skill qualification, required authority, NPC knowledge and willingness, and commitment conflicts must be added only through explicit authored rules and supported state; they are not inferred from relationship scores or fabricated by the generator.

Every generated opportunity should be traceable to the matter, circumstance, event, or authored historical trigger that caused it to exist. The player-facing explanation should describe the actual channel through which it reached the character, rather than use a generic claim of professional reach. Generation and presentation are separate: pacing may determine when an eligible opportunity is surfaced, but it must not rewrite the underlying event, fabricate a deadline, conceal a real deadline, or make an unresolved matter disappear. An empty board or quiet Workday is valid. Do not generate work solely to fill a quota.

Not every NPC need or institutional matter should be converted into player work. Some developments should resolve between NPCs, remain outside the player's knowledge, alter an institution or relationship, or become visible only through later evidence or conversation. Assignments and Challenges remain optional and must never become hidden prerequisites for mandatory Works.

### 15.6 Institutional responses and matter lifecycle

Institutional matters are durable situations, not task cards that disappear when a player submits a report. Filing, assessment, authorization, execution, verification, and resolution are distinct institutional acts. A submitted report is evidence for a decision; it is not itself an institutional decision or authorization.

Consequential institutional responses are recorded as typed world events linked to the originating matter and relevant evidence. A response identifies its actor, institution, appointment or other authority basis, decision, and any authorized scope. It must not be inferred from professional rank, relationship scores, an NPC's private objective, or the mere fact that a report was received. The acting person must plausibly know the evidence being assessed, and their active appointment must grant the capability required for the response.

Assessment, authorization, execution, verification, and resolution are separate stages. An authorization must refer to an appropriate preceding assessment and state its scope. Execution requires a valid authorization or another explicitly recorded basis. Verification must assess the work actually performed against the authorized scope. A matter is resolved only by an explicit, supported institutional response; filing, referral, authorization, or an unverified claim of completion does not resolve it. A referral must identify its receiving person or institution and remain linked to the originating matter and report. Until a destination can be represented and validated, new referral decisions must not be recorded; legacy records remain readable without inventing a recipient. Original records and earlier response events remain part of the historical record.

The first Era I vertical slice uses the North-Bay roof matter. Shuruppak-ilu's Senior Scribe appointment can assess institutional reports. Ur-Nanna's Storehouse Steward appointment can authorize **routine storehouse maintenance within an existing maintenance allocation**, using labor and materials already assigned to ordinary maintenance. This does not grant authority to create new expenditure, release institutional stock, commission major structural work, or determine responsibility beyond the appointment's stated scope. This authority is an authored rule for this fictional Storehouse office, not a claim that a specifically named Late Uruk office with this exact legal remit is directly attested. Archaeological and textual evidence supports differentiated offices and centralized institutional household administration in the Late Uruk period, while the precise internal powers of particular offices remain uncertain. Relevant background includes Hans J. Nissen, “Uruk: Early Administration Practices and the Development of Proto-Cuneiform Writing” (Archéo-Nil 26, 2016), and Vitali Bartash, “Age, Gender and Labor: Recording Human Resources in 3350–2500 BC Mesopotamia” (in *What’s in a Name?*, AOAT 440, 2018). These sources support the broader administrative and labor-allocation context; neither is treated as direct evidence for this fictional Steward’s specific authority.

For the North-Bay maintenance slice, the storehouse roster includes Aru, an authored fictional maintenance worker distinct from Dudu, whose existing repair commission remains limited to its own scope. Ur-Nanna may record an assignment to Aru only after a valid maintenance authorization. The assignment is a typed commitment event; it creates an active worker commitment whose basis is that event and gives the worker the report needed to understand the task. Execution is then recorded as a separate event by the responsible steward, identifying the assigned worker, commitment, exact authorized scope, work performed (which must match that scope), labor basis, materials basis, and result. The execution event fulfills the worker's commitment but does not resolve the institutional matter. Verification must still assess the recorded work, and resolution remains a later explicit institutional act.

Institutional response events must use stable identifiers and reference existing people, appointments, institutions, matters, records, and preceding response events. Save validation must reject dangling references, inactive or mismatched appointments, unsupported authority capabilities, and invalid response sequences. New fields remain optional where necessary to preserve earlier schema-v2 saves; known initial appointments may receive narrowly scoped authored capability migrations.

The world tick remains bounded and deterministic. It may advance a matter based on a recorded authorization, commitment, execution, verification, or an independently scheduled condition. It must not treat the passage of time alone as proof that authorized work was completed. Weather and other external developments may still occur, but their consequences must reflect the matter's actual recorded state.
